Sign-in cards · Hermey

How a bot signs you in to a site without ever being given the password.

Status: built. The three cards (choice, secret, confirm) are asked, answered, consumed and cancelled by the bridge, drawn by both apps, and filled either through the sign-in extension's loopback socket or through the bot screen's DevTools port. What remains is the 1Password service-account backend behind the same interface, and installing the extension into an owner's own Mac browser by hand.

The problem

A bot asked to "log into my Doctolib account" today has two bad options: be handed the password (it ends up in a transcript, a log and a model's context), or be given standing access to a vault (any bot can then read anything). Neither is acceptable, and both are how automation normally does it.

The idea

The bot orchestrates the login. The owner supplies the secret. The two are kept apart: the secret is typed into a card in the Hermey app and flows from the app, through the bridge, into the page. It never enters the conversation, the bot's context, or a log.

Every secret-bearing interaction is a card. There are three kinds.

Card 1 — choice

Shown when a bot reaches a login page and has to know how the owner wants to sign in.

field meaning
site the origin being signed into, e.g. doctolib.de
options the methods the page actually offers: Continue with Google, Email and password, Passkey
note (optional) one line from the bot: what it is trying to do

The bot learns which option was chosen and nothing else.

Card 2 — secret

Shown after a choice, or whenever a value must be typed.

field meaning
site the origin, repeated so a card is never ambiguous
fields one or more masked inputs: username + password, or a single one-time code
fills what the value will be placed into: "the password field on the sign-in form"
expires 2 minutes by default

Rules:

  • Values travel app → bridge → the fill target. They are never returned to the bot, never written to the transcript, never logged, and never stored after the fill.
  • The app records that a login happened and when. Not what was typed.
  • One-time codes are single use and expire with the card.

Card 3 — confirm

Shown when a bot wants to use an existing connection instead of a typed secret.

field meaning
site where the action will happen
connection which saved connection, e.g. "Google — mo@example.com (Composio)"
action one line: "sign in to doctolib.de with this account"

The bot learns yes or no. It never sees the token behind the connection.

The rules that make it safe

  1. Secrets bypass the model. A value entered in a secret card must never be delivered to the bot as a message. If it arrives in the conversation, the whole design has failed.
  2. A bot cannot read the field after the fill. Screenshots are acceptable — password inputs render as dots — but page script execution during a login is not. Either scripts are refused while a secret card is open, or the fill goes through a channel the bot cannot query.
  3. Every card names the site, and a card is only issued for the page the bot is currently on. This is what stops a bot — or something impersonating one — from asking for a secret it has no business holding.
  4. One card at a time, short-lived, single use. No stacking, no reuse, nothing valid after two minutes.
  5. No secret is ever in a URL, a log line, a screenshot filename or an audit event. The audit records the event, the site and the outcome only.
  6. Cancel is always available and always obeyed. A cancelled card fills nothing.

What the bot learns, in full

  • the site it is on,
  • which sign-in method the owner chose,
  • whether the fill succeeded, and the page's own response (wrong password, bad code),
  • for a confirm card, yes or no.

That is the complete list. If a future feature needs more, it goes in this document first.

Fill targets

where the bot works how the fill lands
the bot screen's browser the Hermey Sign-in extension (first choice): it holds a loopback socket to the bridge and types the value into the field, replying only "ok"
the bot screen's browser, launched by the dock the DevTools port, as a fallback when no extension is connected
the owner's Mac browser the same extension, installed by hand; later, the Mac app may fill through its own browser surface
native app windows out of scope for this version; a bot must ask the owner to type

The extension is preferred because it needs no launch flags: it works for a browser the owner started themselves, and it is the same component on the Mac, where launch flags cannot reach at all. It never stores, logs or resends a value, and its reply is an id and a boolean.

When it cannot work

case behaviour
the site offers only a passkey the choice card says so, and the bot asks the owner to complete it on their own device
the app is older than this feature the bot is told plainly to update the app, rather than falling back to asking for a password in chat
the owner does not answer the card expires; the bot reports that it stopped and why
wrong password twice the bot stops and asks, rather than looping

Connecting accounts (Composio, and the same pattern later for others)

Connecting happens in the app, once: the owner taps Connect, finishes the provider's own sign-in, and the connection is stored host-side, per owner, not per bot. A bot then only ever gets card 3 to use it. Revoking is a switch in the app.

The same shape applies to a 1Password service account when the owner has a plan that offers one: the token is entered in the app, scoped to one vault, held by the bridge, and never by a bot. A bot's only door is the card.

Rollout

  1. Mac app: choice card, secret card, OTP card, one real site end to end.
  2. The same cards on iPhone, reusing the bridge contract.
  3. Confirm card and the Composio connection screen.
  4. A 1Password service-account backend behind the identical interface — no new bot-facing concepts.

What must exist before any of this is switched on

  • The bridge route that carries a card answer must not log its body, and must not include it in any audit payload.
  • A test that fails if a secret ever appears in a transcript, a log line or an event payload.
  • A test that a bot cannot obtain the value after the fill.
